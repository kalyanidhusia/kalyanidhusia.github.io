Antibiotics · the bacterial cell wall

Bacteria have a wall. Your cells don't. That's the whole trick.

Bacteria wrap themselves in a mesh wall of peptidoglycan that holds them together against their own internal pressure. Penicillin-class antibiotics block the enzymes that build this wall — the weakened wall then fails and the cell bursts (lysis) under osmotic pressure. Human cells have no wall, which is why these drugs can kill bacteria without killing you: selective toxicity.

After playing you can…
  • Name what the bacterial wall is made of and why losing it bursts the cell.
  • Explain selective toxicity: wall-targeting antibiotics spare human cells, which have no wall.
  • Describe one real resistance mechanism (β-lactamase) and one real counter-move (clavulanate).
  1. 1Bounce the antibiotic off your paddle into the wall — every gold brick is peptidoglycan.

  3. 3Catch a falling CLV capsule (clavulanate): it inhibits β-lactamase for a while, letting your antibiotic break those bricks too.
