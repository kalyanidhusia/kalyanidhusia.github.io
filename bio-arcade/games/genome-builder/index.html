Genome assembly · De Bruijn graphs

You are the assembler — the genome arrives in pieces.

Sequencers cannot read a genome end to end: they return millions of short reads. Assemblers chop every read into k-mers (substrings of length k) and link any two that overlap by k−1 letters — a De Bruijn graph. Walking that graph letter by letter rebuilds the genome. Two things fight you: repeats, where the graph branches and the reads alone cannot say which path the true genome took, and sequencing errors, which show up as k-mers supported by almost no reads, dangling off the graph as dead-end tips.

After playing you can…
  • Define a k-mer and say why consecutive k-mers overlap by k−1 letters.
  • Spot a repeat: two equally supported branches mean the reads cannot resolve the path — and say what extra information would (longer reads).
  • Spot a sequencing error: a barely supported dead-end k-mer, and say what assemblers do with it (prune the tip).
  1. 1The contig grows one k-mer at a time. Each candidate shows how many reads support it — tap the next k-mer that overlaps the contig end by k−1 letters.
  2. 2If one candidate has clearly more read support, trust the coverage: the weak one is a sequencing error.
  3. 3If two candidates are equally supported, do NOT guess — tap Ambiguous. A longer read then arrives and settles the branch. Your first 2 wrong taps are free practice; after that, 3 slips end the run.

Simplified on purpose: we walk k-mers as nodes joined by k−1 overlaps — the textbook De Bruijn formulation makes k-mers the EDGES between (k−1)-mer nodes and finds an Eulerian path. Real assemblers use k ≈ 31–77 over millions of reads, prune tips and pop bubbles statistically, and never know the true genome in advance (here it referees your taps). The overlap rule, the repeat ambiguity, and coverage-as-evidence are the true parts.
