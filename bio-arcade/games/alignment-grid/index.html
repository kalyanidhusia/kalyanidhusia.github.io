Needleman–Wunsch · global alignment

You are the algorithm — fill the table, then walk back.

How similar are two protein sequences? Trying every possible alignment explodes exponentially. In 1970 Needleman & Wunsch showed the best global (end-to-end) alignment falls out of one table filled cell by cell: each cell holds the best score aligning the first letters of A with the first letters of B, and it only needs its three finished neighbors — diagonal (pair the letters, match +2 / mismatch −1), from above or from the left (a gap, −2). The bottom-right cell is the optimal score, and walking the arrows back reconstructs the alignment itself. This is dynamic programming — the same idea that powers route planners and spell-checkers.

After playing you can…
  • Say what one cell of the matrix means, and name the three neighbors it is computed from.
  • Explain what the diagonal move is (pairing two letters) versus the up/left moves (gaps).
  • Explain what the traceback recovers, and why Needleman–Wunsch is exact and global — unlike BLASTP's fast local search.
